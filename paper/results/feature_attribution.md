## 3.3 Feature attribution

SHAP was used for feature attribution, helping us find which features had the biggest influence on model predictions on a global/overall level. found that target-side mean frp was the strongest contributor to transferqability predictions. the next three highest features all had to do with standard deviation of environmental conditions (max temp, surface soil water, frp std's). one plausible explanation for this effect is that some ecosystems can be 'contained' within another where one region's environment is a subset of another, meaning a model trained on the 'inner ecosystem' may perform poorly when applied to the 'outer' ecosystem (extrapolation), while a model trained on the more complex ecosystem can perform well when applied to the inner ecosystem (interpolation). the variability of these environmental features is an indicator of this effect. also, all of these top 4 features were target-side features, meaning that the model valued knowing the target domain's environment over the source domain.

i also looked into how the distance vs bias terms contributed to the final predictions. any i,j cell in the transfer matrix can be reconstructed using (-dist^2(e_i,e_j) + src_bias_i + tgt_bias_j). i separated the distance from bias terms into two different matrices to see how they each contributed to the final predictions. i created a distance matrix, where i took the negative squared distance between all (i,j) pairs. i also created a bias matrix, where each i,j cell is the sum of i's source bias term and j's target bias term. i then tested the correlation of these matrices against the ground truth transfer matrix, finding: dist term alone spearman vs true: 0.4778, and bias term alone spearman vs true: 0.4907. From this I could see that both the embeddings and biases were contributing almost equally to the final prediction, which is good. 

I also looked into the standard deviations for the bias matrix vs the embedding distance matrix, finding that dist matrix std was 0.0283 and bias matrix std was 0.0309. there was a <10% difference between the spread of these terms, showing no sign of the transferability model collapsing onto a single term, indicating that both parts of the domain representations - the biases and the embeddings - contributed similarly to the final prediction.

SHAP was used to quantify the contribution of individual domain descriptors to transferability predictions. Target-domain mean FRP was the strongest contributor, followed by the standard deviations of maximum temperature, surface soil water, and FRP. All four of the highest-ranked features were target-side descriptors, indicating that the transferability model placed greater predictive weight on characteristics of the target environment than on corresponding source-domain characteristics.

The prominence of variability measures suggests that the range of environmental conditions within a domain may be important for determining transferability. One possible explanation is that some ecosystems contain the environmental conditions of another as a subset. A model trained in the less variable, "inner" environment may need to extrapolate when applied to the more heterogeneous environment, whereas a model trained in the broader environment can interpolate when applied to the narrower one. Higher within-domain variability could therefore indicate a domain that encompasses a wider range of conditions, creating directional differences in transferability.

V4 predictions can be separated into an embedding-distance component and two directional bias components. For each source-target pair, the embedding component is the negated squared distance between the two domain embeddings, while the bias component is the sum of the source bias for the source domain and the target bias for the target domain. To determine whether the final predictions were driven primarily by embedding geometry or by the directional biases, these components were separated into independent matrices and compared with the empirical transfer matrix.

Both components showed similar relationships with the empirical transfer matrix. The embedding-distance matrix had a Spearman correlation of 0.4778 with the ground truth, while the bias matrix had a correlation of 0.4907. The comparable correlations indicate that the embedding geometry and directional biases contributed at similar levels to the transferability prediction rather than one component dominating the other.

Their relative contribution was also reflected in their distributions. The embedding-distance matrix had a standard deviation of 0.0283, compared with 0.0309 for the bias matrix, a difference of less than 10%. Neither component therefore collapsed toward a nearly constant value, indicating that both the learned domain geometry and the directional biases contributed substantial variation to the final predictions.
